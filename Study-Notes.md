# Exam facts you should lock in first

- **Test name:** Unmanned Aircraft General – Small (`UAG`)
- **Format:** 60 scored multiple-choice questions, plus typically 5 unscored validation questions
- **Time:** 2 hours
- **Passing score:** 70% (42 of 60)
- **Age to take the test:** 14; **age to hold the certificate:** 16
- **Retake wait:** 14 calendar days after a failure
- **Typical test-center fee:** about $175 per attempt via PSI
- **Certificate fee:** none. After you pass, you apply in IACRA; TSA security vetting follows; then a temporary certificate, then the plastic card by mail
- **Currency after you pass:** free online recurrent training every 24 calendar months (`ALC-677`, or `ALC-515` if you also hold a current Part 61 flight review)
- **What you get at the testing center:** the Airman Knowledge Testing Supplement (`FAA-CT-8080-2H`) with sectional-chart excerpts, legends, weather figures, and airport diagrams. Many missed questions come from not knowing how to use that booklet.

Approximate ACS weighting used by most current prep materials:

| Area | Typical share |
|---|---|
| I. Regulations | 15–25% |
| II. Airspace & operating requirements | 15–25% |
| III. Weather | 11–16% |
| IV. Loading & performance | 7–11% |
| V. Operations (radio, airports, emergencies, ADM, physiology, maintenance, night) | 35–45% |

Operations is the largest bucket. Do not under-study charts, airports, night ops, CRM, and emergencies just because they sit later in a syllabus.

## Core skill prerequisites (expanded)

These are not “nice extras.” They are the skills the test uses to ask every other question.

**Spatial orientation and mental visualization**
- Convert a 2-D sectional into 3-D airspace: floors, ceilings, shelves, rings, and extensions.
- Visualize an aircraft’s position relative to you, a runway, a Class D surface area, and a nearby Class B shelf.
- Read latitude/longitude, statute-mile scale bars, and magnetic variation without guessing.
- Translate “400 ft AGL vs. 400 ft above a structure within a 400-ft radius” into a mental cylinder around a building or tower.

**Meteorological interpretation**
- Decode METAR/TAF groups: wind, visibility, weather phenomena, sky condition, temperature/dew point, altimeter.
- Know what ceiling means (lowest broken or overcast layer) versus scattered clouds.
- Connect density altitude to lift, battery endurance, and climb performance.
- Recognize frontal weather, wind shear, mechanical turbulence, and thunderstorm life cycle as go/no-go factors.

**Risk management and ADM**
- Use a repeatable process before every flight, not vibes.
- Separate *hazard* (a condition) from *risk* (probability × severity).
- Identify pressure to “just get the shot,” fatigue, medication, and client demands as accident precursors.

**Regulatory navigation**
- Read a regulation as written: definitions first, then operating rules, then exceptions and waivable sections.
- Cross-reference Part 107 with Parts 47/48 (registration), 89 (Remote ID), 71 (airspace), AIM, and NOTAMs.
- Know the difference between a **waiver** (deviate from a Part 107 rule) and an **airspace authorization** (enter controlled airspace under §107.41).

## 1. Regulations — 14 CFR Parts 107, 47, 48, and 89

### 1.1 Who Part 107 applies to
- Civil small unmanned aircraft operations in the United States.
- **Small unmanned aircraft:** less than **55 pounds** at takeoff, including everything on board or attached.
- **Small UAS:** the aircraft plus the associated elements needed for safe operation (control station, links, etc.).
- **Does not apply to:** air carrier operations; operations under the recreational exception in 49 U.S.C. 44809; operations flown under a 333/44807 exemption when the operator elects the exemption; or Part 91 operations of an sUAS that has an airworthiness certificate.

Key definitions you must be able to state:
- **Unmanned aircraft:** operated without direct human intervention from within or on the aircraft.
- **Control station:** the interface used by the remote pilot to control the aircraft.
- **Visual observer (VO):** a person designated by the remote PIC to help see and avoid traffic and objects.
- **Remote PIC:** the person directly responsible for and the final authority over the operation.
- **Person manipulating the controls:** may be someone other than the remote PIC, but only if the remote PIC can immediately take over.

### 1.2 Remote Pilot Certificate
Eligibility:
- At least 16 years old
- Read, speak, write, and understand English
- Physical and mental condition to safely operate
- Pass the UAG test *or*, if you already hold a Part 61 pilot certificate (other than student) and a flight review in the previous 24 months, complete the free `ALC-451` course instead

Other certification rules:
- Certificate and identification must be readily available during operations.
- Falsification, reproduction, or alteration of a certificate, rating, authorization, record, or report can mean denial, suspension, revocation, and criminal exposure.
- Alcohol/drug offenses and refusing a drug/alcohol test can bar or revoke certification.
- Aeronautical knowledge recency: complete the required online recurrent training within the previous **24 calendar months**.
- You may allow another person to manipulate the controls only if you, as remote PIC, can immediately regain control. You remain responsible.

### 1.3 Registration — Part 47 vs. Part 48
- **Part 107 operations require registration of the aircraft, including aircraft under 0.55 lb.** The recreational under-0.55-lb exception does **not** apply to Part 107.
- **Part 48 (FAADroneZone):** typical path for sUAS under 55 lb. About **$5 per aircraft**, valid **3 years**, unique registration number marked on the exterior.
- **Part 47:** required for aircraft **55 lb or more**, certain trust/ownership situations, and some N-number / international-use cases.
- Marking must be durable and visible; carry proof of registration when you fly.
- Recreational registration is per-owner; Part 107 registration is **per aircraft**. Do not mix the two concepts on the test.

### 1.4 Remote PIC authority and preflight duties (§107.19, §107.49)
The remote PIC:
- Has final authority and responsibility
- Must ensure the sUAS is in a condition for safe operation
- Must discontinue flight if it becomes unsafe
- Must be able to see the aircraft or use a VO so someone can determine location, attitude, altitude, and direction of flight
- Must assess operating environment: local weather, local airspace, flight restrictions, location of persons and property, ground hazards
- Must inspect the system: aircraft, control station, links, available power, payload security
- Must ensure all required documents and equipment are on hand
- Must ensure all persons directly participating are informed of operating conditions, emergency procedures, contingency procedures, roles, and potential hazards

Medical rule (§107.17): you may not operate if you know or have reason to know of a physical or mental condition that would interfere with safe operation. There is **no FAA medical certificate** for Part 107, but that does not mean “any condition is fine.”

### 1.5 Hard operating limits (§107.51 and related)
Memorize these exactly:

- **Max groundspeed:** 87 knots / 100 mph
- **Max altitude:** 400 ft AGL, **unless** flown within a **400-ft radius of a structure** and not more than **400 ft above that structure’s immediate uppermost limit**
- **Min visibility:** 3 statute miles, measured from the **control station**
- **Cloud clearance:** at least **500 ft below** and **2,000 ft horizontally**
- Visibility definition for this part: average slant range from the control station at which prominent unlighted objects can be identified by day and prominent lighted objects by night.

Other core operating rules:
- **VLOS (§107.31):** unaided vision (corrective lenses allowed; binoculars/FPV do not satisfy VLOS). You or a VO must be able to determine the aircraft’s location, attitude, altitude, and direction of flight, and scan for traffic/hazards.
- **VO (§107.33):** if used, the remote PIC, person at the controls, and VO must maintain effective communication; the VO may not use a camera/binoculars as a substitute for unaided VLOS; they must be able to see the aircraft and airspace well enough to provide collision-avoidance information.
- **One sUAS at a time (§107.35):** a person may not act as remote PIC, person at the controls, or VO for more than one sUAS at the same time.
- **No hazardous materials (§107.36)**
- **Right-of-way (§107.37):** the small UA must yield to all other aircraft; do not operate so close as to create a collision hazard.
- **Airports (§107.43):** do not interfere with airport operations, traffic patterns, or incoming/outgoing aircraft.
- **Prohibited/restricted areas (§107.45):** need permission from the using/controlling agency.
- **TFRs / NOTAM restrictions (§107.47):** comply with flight restrictions published by NOTAM (VIP, disaster, stadium, wildfire, etc.).
- **No careless or reckless operation (§107.23)**
- **No dropping an object** that creates an undue hazard to persons or property
- **No carriage of property of another for compensation across state lines** under Part 107 in a way that would make it interstate air transportation outside the rule’s limits; in-state transport is allowed if total weight stays under 55 lb and other rules are met
- **External load** is allowed if securely attached and does not adversely affect flight characteristics or controllability
- **Moving vehicle/aircraft (§107.25):** no operation from a moving aircraft. From a moving land or water vehicle only over a sparsely populated area and not transporting property for compensation/hire, unless waived
- **Alcohol/drugs (§107.27):** 8-hour bottle-to-throttle concept is tested through Part 91/61 knowledge culture and Part 107’s “no alcohol/drugs that affect safety” rule; also no operation while under the influence or within 8 hours after consuming alcohol as applied in standard FAA testing knowledge. Know that a BAC of 0.04% or more, or any measurable impairing drug, is disqualifying in FAA alcohol rules that the test expects you to recognize.
- **Transponder / ADS-B Out:** Part 107 aircraft are **prohibited** from using ATC transponders and ADS-B Out unless otherwise authorized. Remote ID is a separate broadcast requirement under Part 89.
- **In-flight emergency (§107.21):** the remote PIC may deviate from Part 107 to the extent necessary to meet the emergency, then report if the Administrator requests.

### 1.6 Night and civil twilight (§107.29)
Current rule, not the old “daylight only unless waived” version:

- Night operations are allowed if:
  1. The remote PIC completed an initial knowledge test or recurrent training **after April 6, 2021** that includes night operations, **and**
  2. The sUAS has **anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles** with a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision
- Civil twilight also requires anti-collision lighting visible 3 SM
- The remote PIC may reduce lighting intensity if necessary for collision avoidance
- Night does **not** relax VLOS, airspace authorization, operations-over-people, or weather minima
- Know physiological night issues: dark adaptation (~30 minutes), off-center viewing, empty-field myopia, autokinesis, false horizons.

### 1.7 Operations over people and moving vehicles
Default rule (§107.39): do not fly over a person unless that person is:
- Directly participating in the operation, or
- Located under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle that can provide reasonable protection from a falling small UA, or
- The operation fits Category 1–4 in Subpart D

**“Over”** means over any part of a person, even briefly.

Categories (high-yield):

**Category 1**
- 0.55 lb or less throughout the operation, including everything attached
- No exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin
- Sustained flight over open-air assemblies requires Remote ID compliance under Part 89

**Category 2**
- Eligible aircraft listed on an accepted Declaration of Compliance
- Does not cause injury equivalent to a transfer of **11 ft-lb** of kinetic energy or greater upon impact
- No exposed rotating parts that would lacerate skin
- Remote PIC operating instructions, labeling, and maintenance requirements apply
- Sustained flight over open-air assemblies has additional limits unless closed/restricted site or Remote ID conditions are met

**Category 3**
- Similar injury threshold to Category 2 (**11 ft-lb**) and no lacerating exposed rotors
- **More operational limits:** generally not over open-air assemblies; typically limited to closed- or restricted-access sites where people have notice, **or** no sustained flight over people

**Category 4**
- Aircraft has an **airworthiness certificate** under Part 21
- Operated in accordance with the approved Flight Manual / operating limitations, which must not prohibit the intended over-people operation

**Moving vehicles (§107.145)**
- Must fit a valid category
- For Categories 1–3: remain within/over a closed- or restricted-access site with notice to people in vehicles, **or** do not maintain **sustained flight** over moving vehicles
- Category 4 follows the certificated aircraft’s operating limitations

Also know:
- Means of Compliance (MoC) and Declaration of Compliance (DoC)
- Labeling for Category 2 and 3
- Effect of modifications: changing the aircraft can void eligibility
- Maintenance must keep the aircraft in the declared condition
- Variable mode/configuration aircraft must meet the category they are actually flying in at that moment.

### 1.8 Remote ID — Part 89
The ACS tests this. Know the operating concept, not just the slogan “drones need Remote ID.”

- **Standard Remote ID aircraft:** broadcast required message elements from takeoff to shutdown
- **Remote ID broadcast module:** add-on module; typically requires the aircraft to remain within VLOS of the person manipulating the controls
- **FRIA (FAA-Recognized Identification Area):** a location where RID broadcast may not be required for certain operations; **do not assume Part 107 commercial work can ignore RID** just because FRIAs exist
- Message elements generally include a unique identifier, location/altitude of the UA, location of the control station or takeoff point (depending on standard vs module), velocity, emergency status, and time mark
- Product labeling / serial number used for RID
- Confirmation of identification / production requirements
- ADS-B Out is **not** a substitute for Remote ID, and Part 107 still prohibits ADS-B Out unless authorized
- Preflight: verify the RID system is functioning before flight.

### 1.9 Safety event / accident reporting (§107.9)
Report to the FAA within **10 calendar days** if the operation involves:
- **Serious injury to any person or any loss of consciousness**, or
- **Damage to property other than the small UA**, unless:
  - repair cost (parts + labor) does **not exceed $500**, or
  - fair market value of the property in a total loss does **not exceed $500**

The drone’s own damage does not by itself trigger the report. “Serious injury requiring hospitalization” is the common study-guide paraphrase; the regulation’s actual trigger is **serious injury or loss of consciousness**. Learn the regulation’s wording.

### 1.10 Inspections, records, compliance
- Make the sUAS available to the FAA for inspection or testing
- Produce records and documents upon request
- Keep required records; know that maintenance/inspection records matter especially for Category 2/3/4 aircraft
- Previously manufactured aircraft and multiple-category aircraft have specific eligibility/record issues on the ACS

### 1.11 Waivers (§107.200 / §107.205)
A waiver is not an airspace authorization.

Commonly waivable sections:
- §107.25 moving vehicle/aircraft
- §107.29 anti-collision light provisions for night/twilight
- §107.31 VLOS (not for carriage of property of another for compensation/hire)
- §107.33 visual observer
- §107.35 multiple sUAS
- §107.37(a) right-of-way
- §107.39 operations over people
- §107.41 certain airspace
- §107.51 operating limitations (speed, altitude, visibility, cloud clearance)
- §107.145 operations over moving vehicles

You must show the FAA the operation can be conducted safely under alternative mitigations. Apply through the current FAA waiver portal (Aviation Safety Hub / process in effect at the time). Airspace authorizations still go through LAANC or FAADroneZone.

## 2. Airspace classification and operating requirements

### 2.1 The National Airspace System
- ATC’s job is separation and traffic flow in controlled airspace; you still must see and avoid.
- Class A is 18,000 ft MSL and above; Part 107 aircraft almost never go there, but know it exists so chart questions do not confuse you.
- Authorization under §107.41 is required before operating in **Class B, C, D, or Class E surface area**. Class G does not need ATC authorization solely because of class.

### 2.2 Controlled and uncontrolled airspace

**Class B**
- Around the busiest airports
- Typically surface to 10,000 ft MSL, but **always read the chart**
- Solid **blue** lines; each shelf is boxed with floor/ceiling like `70/30` meaning 7,000 ft ceiling / 3,000 ft floor MSL
- Manned aircraft: clearance to enter
- sUAS: prior authorization

**Class C**
- Solid **magenta** lines
- Typical core: surface to 4,000 ft above airport elevation, plus a 10 NM shelf from 1,200 ft AGL to 4,000 ft AAE — **typical, not universal**
- Manned aircraft: two-way comms and usually a Mode C transponder/ADS-B
- sUAS: prior authorization in the Class C airspace itself

**Class D**
- Dashed **blue** line
- Typically surface to 2,500 ft AAE
- Towered airport
- sUAS: prior authorization for the Class D surface area

**Class E**
- Controlled airspace that is not B/C/D
- Common floors: **700 ft AGL** (shaded magenta vignette) or **1,200 ft AGL** (blue vignette)
- **Class E surface area:** dashed **magenta** line around some airports; this surface E **does** require Part 107 authorization
- Class E starting at 700/1,200 AGL does **not** by itself ban flying at 200 ft AGL in the Class G underneath

**Class G**
- Uncontrolled
- Often surface up to 700 or 1,200 ft AGL
- No ATC authorization merely because it is G
- Weather minima in Part 107 still apply (3 SM, 500/2,000 from clouds)

### 2.3 Special use and other airspace
Know which require authorization or extreme caution:

| Type | Chart cue / concept | Need permission? |
|---|---|---|
| Prohibited (P-) | Solid blue hashed box | Yes. Do not enter. |
| Restricted (R-) | Blue hashed | Yes, from controlling agency when “hot” |
| Warning (W-) | Offshore | Caution; often military
| MOA | Magenta hashed | No discrete sUAS “clearance,” but high-speed military traffic; check status |
| Alert area | Magenta hashed | High training activity; caution |
| CFA | Not charted | Not depicted; stay alert |
| NSA | Heavy dashed magenta | Chart note may request voluntary avoidance |
| TFR | NOTAM, not a standing chart symbol | Mandatory compliance |
| MTR (IR/VR) | Gray lines with IR/VR numbers | Fast low-level military traffic; wider route if four-digit / above 1,500 AGL conventions |
| Wildlife refuge / national park notes | Chart notes | Often “request remain above 2,000 AGL” for manned; still check park/FAA rules for UAS |
| Parachute jump | Chart symbol | High-density descending traffic |
| TRSA | Optional radar service | Not a class of airspace |
| Stadium / VIP / disaster / wildfire TFRs | NOTAMs | Common Part 107 traps |

### 2.4 TFRs, NOTAMs, LAANC, DroneZone
- **NOTAM:** Time-critical notice. Check before every flight. Sources include 1800wxbrief, FAA NOTAM search, and UAS apps that ingest official data. Apps can lag; official NOTAMs win.
- **TFR reasons:** VIP movements, space flight, stadium events (often 3 NM / 3,000 ft AGL for major sporting events), disaster relief, wildfire suppression, airborne hazards.
- **LAANC:** Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability. Near-real-time automated authorization in many controlled-airspace grids, up to published altitude ceilings in each grid.
- If LAANC cannot approve the requested altitude or location, use **FAADroneZone** for a further authorization request.
- Authorization ≠ waiver. You can have LAANC approval and still be illegal if you break VLOS, weather, or over-people rules.
- Night authorizations are available in many facilities, but you still need night-qualified training and 3-SM anti-collision lighting.

### 2.5 Chart literacy (this is where first-time testers fail)
You must read a sectional cold.

**Coordinates and measurement**
- Latitude: parallels, N/S; each tick mark commonly 1 minute
- Longitude: meridians, E/W
- 1 degree latitude ≈ 60 NM
- Sectional scale is 1:500,000
- Tick marks and the scale bar are how you measure distance to an airport or airspace ring

**Airport symbols**
- Magenta airport: no control tower / part-time notes matter
- Blue airport: control tower exists (check hours in Chart Supplement)
- Tick marks around the airport circle: fuel or services conventions
- Star: rotating beacon
- “C” in a circle: common traffic advisory / UNICOM information nearby
- Numbers beside airport: field elevation and longest runway length in hundreds of feet (example: `126 / 81` style pairing of elevation and runway)
- Lighting asterisks and notes: pilot-controlled lighting, limitations
- Military airports, private airports (Pvt), heliports, ultralight parks, seaplane bases

**Obstacles and terrain**
- Individual towers: elevation MSL and AGL in parentheses, e.g. `1504 (499)`
- Lighted obstacles vs. unlighted
- Group obstruction symbols
- **MEF (Maximum Elevation Figure):** large blue two-digit number in each quadrangle; thousands and hundreds of MSL covering the highest terrain/obstacle plus a buffer. Used to understand the highest known object in that grid, not as a Part 107 altitude clearance card.
- Contour lines, tinting for terrain, and visual checkpoints (magenta flags)

**Airspace print language**
- Solid blue = Class B
- Solid magenta = Class C
- Dashed blue = Class D
- Dashed magenta = Class E to the surface
- Magenta vignette = Class E starts at 700 AGL
- Blue vignette = Class E starts at 1,200 AGL
- Class G is whatever remains under those floors

**Magnetic variation**
- Isogonic lines show degrees E or W variation
- True vs magnetic: magnetic = true minus easterly / plus westerly in the common memory phrase, but **use the local isogonic value on the chart**, not a slogan
- Runway numbers are magnetic

**Other chart items the test loves**
- Victor airways and GPS routes (you will not fly them, but they explain traffic)
- Special conservation notes
- Mode C veil around Class B (generally 30 NM): relevant to manned transponder rules; sUAS still uses Part 107 authorization logic rather than “I have a veil so I can fly”)
- Chart Supplement / Airport/Facility Directory as the source for tower hours, frequencies, preferred runways, and remarks

## 3. Weather and meteorology

### 3.1 Sources
- Official briefing: 1800wxbrief / Flight Service
- METAR, TAF, AIRMET, SIGMET, Convective SIGMET
- PIREPs
- Winds and temperatures aloft
- Graphical Forecasts for Aviation (GFA)
- Weather charts: surface analysis, prognostic charts, radar summary, satellite
- ASOS / AWOS on airport frequencies
- Internet sources are allowed for planning, but you must understand aviation-formatted products because that is what the test uses

### 3.2 METAR / TAF decoding
Be fluent, not approximate.

METAR skeleton:
`METAR KABC 121856Z 18012G20KT 3SM -RA BR BKN008 OVC015 12/11 A2984 RMK...`

Know:
- Station and time (Zulu)
- Wind: direction **from** which it blows, in degrees true on METAR; gusts
- Visibility in statute miles
- Weather codes: `-RA`, `TS`, `BR`, `FG`, `HZ`, `SN`, `SHRA`, `VCSH`, etc.
- Sky: `FEW`, `SCT`, `BKN`, `OVC` + hundreds of feet AGL
- Ceiling = lowest BKN or OVC
- Temperature / dew point (°C)
- Altimeter (`A2984` = 29.84 inHg)
- Remarks: A02, SLP, T-group, lightning, thunderstorm location

TAF:
- Valid periods
- `FM`, `BECMG`, `TEMPO`, `PROB30`
- Wind, visibility, weather, clouds changing over 24–30 hours
- Use the TAF for the airport nearest/most representative of your site, then adjust for local terrain

### 3.3 Atmosphere and performance
- Pressure: highs = descending, more stable, better vis; lows = rising, clouds, precipitation
- Standard atmosphere: 15°C / 29.92 inHg at sea level; roughly 1 inHg per 1,000 ft
- Density altitude rises with hot + high + humid + low pressure
- High density altitude = less lift, less prop/rotor efficiency, higher true airspeed for a given indicated performance, shorter hover margins, faster battery drain for the same flight profile
- Wind gradient / surface friction: wind usually stronger and more veered with altitude
- Mechanical turbulence: buildings, ridgelines, trees
- Thermal turbulence: uneven heating
- Wind shear: change in speed or direction; microburst is the extreme case
- Mountain effects: rotors, standing waves, up/down drafts on windward/leeward slopes

### 3.4 Air masses, fronts, clouds
- Cold front: steep, fast, towering CU/CB, narrow band, gusty winds, after-front clearing and cooler air
- Warm front: slope, stratiform clouds, steady precip, poor vis, possible icing in manned profiles
- Stationary / occluded fronts: lingering weather
- Stable air: smooth, layered clouds, poor vis in haze/fog
- Unstable air: turbulence, showery precip, good vis between showers, towering clouds

Cloud types worth knowing:
- Stratus / nimbostratus
- Cumulus / towering cumulus / cumulonimbus
- Fog types: radiation, advection, upslope, precipitation-induced, steam

### 3.5 Thunderstorms and other hazards
Three stages:
1. **Cumulus** — updrafts, growing tower
2. **Mature** — updrafts and downdrafts, rain, lightning, hail, gust front, possible microburst
3. **Dissipating** — mostly downdrafts

Hazards: lightning, hail, icing, extreme turbulence, microbursts, tornadoes, rapid wind shifts.

Other testable hazards:
- Icing (even sUAS rotors/props and pitot-like sensors suffer)
- Hail
- Lightning even miles from the cell
- Fog reducing the 3 SM visibility minimum
- Low ceilings violating 500-ft-below-cloud
- Wildfire smoke / volcanic ash
- Temperature inversions and poor visibility

Go/no-go rule of thumb the FAA wants: if you cannot maintain 3 SM visibility and cloud clearance from the control station and along the route, you do not launch. “It looks fine over the field” is not enough if a cell is moving in.

## 4. Loading, performance, and aircraft systems

### 4.1 Weight, balance, CG
- Total weight = aircraft + batteries + payload + mounting hardware
- Exceeding max takeoff weight reduces reserve power and may make the aircraft unairworthy under “condition for safe operation”
- CG too far forward or aft: pitch instability, motor overload, reduced control authority, shorter endurance
- Lateral imbalance from a camera hanging off one side
- Adding a broadcast module, strobe, or payload changes both weight and CG
- Any load must be secure; a shifting payload is both a performance problem and a dropping-object problem

### 4.2 Performance variables
Know how each factor changes takeoff, hover, climb, and endurance:
- Weight
- Density altitude
- Wind (headwind vs tailwind vs gusty mechanical turbulence)
- Slope / ground effect near structures
- Battery state of charge and temperature
- Manufacturer performance data, if provided — use it; do not invent numbers

### 4.3 Batteries
High-yield lithium battery knowledge:
- High energy density, fire risk
- Thermal runaway: once started, hard to stop; toxic smoke
- Safe charging: correct charger, fireproof bag/container, do not charge unattended on combustibles
- Storage at manufacturer storage voltage, cool dry place
- Inspect for swelling, odor, damaged wraps, dented cells
- Cold batteries: reduced capacity and possible sudden voltage sag
- Hot batteries: degraded life and higher fire risk
- Voltage sag under high load at high density altitude
- Transport: spare lithium batteries have special handling concerns; damaged batteries are a hazard
- After a crash, treat the pack as a delayed fire risk

### 4.4 RF, C2 link, GPS, automation
- Control-and-command link is line-of-sight RF, not magic
- 2.4 GHz: longer range, more congestion / better obstacle penetration in some cases
- 5.8 GHz: often used for video; more bandwidth, more easily blocked
- Interference: Wi-Fi, cell sites, power lines, cameras, other pilots
- Multipath near buildings
- Antenna orientation
- Lost-link / RTH behavior: know what *your* aircraft will do — hover, land, RTH to last GPS home
- GPS loss: RTH may fail or drift; attitude-only mode increases workload
- Flyaway: lost control with continuing flight; have an emergency plan (alert people, consider ATC/airport notification if near an airport)
- Automation does not transfer PIC responsibility
- Frequency congestion at events is a common lost-link setup

### 4.5 Maintenance and airworthiness philosophy
Part 107 has no Part 21 airworthiness certificate for ordinary Category 1–3 aircraft, but:
- The remote PIC determines “condition for safe operation”
- Follow manufacturer instructions
- Inspect props, motors, arms, compass, IMU, antennas, landing gear, camera mount, control station, firmware
- After any abnormal landing, inspect before next flight
- Category 2/3/4 aircraft have extra maintenance and configuration-control duties
- Recordkeeping: what was done, by whom, when
- Anyone may perform maintenance on a typical Part 107 sUAS, but the remote PIC is still responsible for the result

## 5. Operations — the biggest exam slice

### 5.1 Radio communications
You are not required to talk to ATC for a standard LAANC hop the way a manned VFR pilot calls “ready for departure,” but the test expects manned-aviation radio literacy so you can monitor and not create a conflict.

Know:
- Phonetic alphabet: Alpha through Zulu
- How to state altitudes, headings, time
- CTAF vs UNICOM vs ATIS vs ground vs tower vs approach
- Towered vs nontowered procedures
- Self-announce script at nontowered fields: who you are, where you are, what you intend
- How manned pilots report “left downwind 17,” “short final,” “departing 35”
- Monitor the frequency when operating near an airport even if you are not talking
- Call signs / N-numbers vs your drone registration number; do not invent confusing phraseology
- If ATC issues instructions under an authorization, comply or break off the operation

### 5.2 Airport operations
- Traffic pattern: upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, final; standard is left traffic unless charted otherwise
- Pattern altitude is typically 1,000 ft AGL for piston airplanes, 1,500 AGL for many turbines, 500–800 for rotorcraft — meaning a drone at 400 AGL can still conflict on final, departure, and helicopter routes
- Runway markings: threshold, centerline, aiming point, displaced threshold, blast pad / stopway, hold-short lines
- Signs: location (red/black), direction, mandatory hold-short
- Lighting: REIL, VASI/PAPI, taxiway edge/center, rotating beacon (civil: white-green; military: white-white-green; lighted heliport: white-green-yellow)
- Pilot-controlled lighting
- Seaplane bases and heliports
- SIDA: Security Identification Display Area at commercial airports; do not wander airside
- Chart Supplement is the source for hours, frequencies, remarks (“birds,” “ultralights,” “parachute,” tower part-time)
- When the tower closes, Class D often reverts to E or G; read the chart/Supplement
- Wildlife hazards and strike reporting (FAA wildlife strike system)

### 5.3 Emergency procedures
Build a script for:
- Lost link
- Flyaway
- Low battery / battery failure
- Control-station failure
- GPS loss
- Magnetic interference / compass error
- Aircraft in a tree or on a rooftop
- Aircraft drifting toward people or an active runway
- Lithium fire on the ground
- Midair conflict: the sUAS yields; descend/land/move away
- Operations over people going wrong
- Night emergency: lighting failure means the night condition may no longer be satisfied — land
- Who you call: local ATC if near an airport and the aircraft is heading into traffic; 911 for ground injuries; FAA report if §107.9 thresholds are met

### 5.4 ADM, CRM, hazardous attitudes
**IMSAFE**
- Illness
- Medication
- Stress
- Alcohol
- Fatigue
- Emotion / Eating (both appear in teaching materials; know the safety idea)

**Five hazardous attitudes and antidotes**
- Anti-authority → “Follow the rules. They are usually right.”
- Impulsivity → “Not so fast. Think first.”
- Invulnerability → “It could happen to me.”
- Macho → “Taking chances is foolish.”
- Resignation → “I can make a difference / I am not helpless.”

**DECIDE**
Detect → Estimate → Choose → Identify → Do → Evaluate

**3P / PAVE / CARE** models also appear in PHAK-derived questions:
- PAVE: Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External pressures
- CARE / TEAM for risk controls

**CRM**
- Brief the VO before launch
- Assign who scans which sector
- Closed-loop communication: send, repeat, confirm
- Sterile “cockpit” during launch, recovery, and when traffic is near
- The remote PIC still owns the decision even if a client is yelling

### 5.5 Human factors and physiology
- Fatigue is a leading human-factor accident cause
- Dehydration and heat illness at outdoor sites
- Hyperventilation
- Stress narrowing attention
- Medications: antihistamines, sleep aids, narcotics, some prescription meds; “if the label says may cause drowsiness, you should not fly”
- Vision:
  - Photopic (day), mesopic, scotopic (night)
  - Rods vs cones
  - Night scan: off-center viewing
  - Empty-field myopia
  - Autokinesis
  - Glare from landing lights or camera strobes
- Dark adaptation takes about 30 minutes and is ruined quickly by white light
- Hypoxia is less central for 400-ft AGL drone work than for manned high altitude, but the test still expects you to recognize it as a physiological factor
- Fitness for flight is a remote PIC duty, not a formality

### 5.6 Night operations as an operations subject
Beyond the regulation itself, expect questions on:
- Anti-collision light purpose and 3 SM visibility of that light
- Why unlighted towers and wires are worse at night
- Manned aircraft lighting: red left, green right, white rear; landing lights
- How to pick a launch site with a visual horizon and safe abort area
- Preflight inspection items unique to night: lights, spare batteries, lighting of the control station without wrecking night vision

## 6. The process after studying

1. Create an **IACRA** account and get your **FTN** before scheduling.
2. Schedule `UAG` at [faa.psiexams.com](https://faa.psiexams.com).
3. Bring acceptable government photo ID. Know your FTN.
4. Use the testing supplement; do not try to memorize every chart in the country.
5. Pass → wait for the exam ID to appear in IACRA (can take up to 48 hours) → file Form **8710-13**.
6. TSA vetting → print temporary certificate → fly commercially once that temporary is in hand and the aircraft is registered.
7. Put recurrent training on a 24-month reminder.

## Why the FAA built the test this way

Part 107 is not a camera-operator quiz. It is an entry ticket into the same NAS used by airliners, air ambulances, crop-dusters, and student pilots in the pattern.

- **Shared language:** sectional symbols, METAR groups, CTAF calls, and airspace names let a remote pilot understand what a manned pilot is about to do at 300–1,000 ft.
- **Predictable limits:** 400 ft, 100 mph, 3 SM, 500/2,000 cloud clearance, and VLOS exist so other people can roughly predict where small UA will be.
- **Proactive risk control:** IMSAFE, PAVE, and DECIDE exist because most drone mishaps are decision failures, not mysterious physics.
- **Scalable commerce:** Categories 1–4, waivers, LAANC, and Remote ID are how the FAA tries to allow economic use without giving every operator unlimited access to people and controlled airspace.

If you study only memorized numbers, you can still fail chart and ADM questions. If you study only “how my drone flies,” you will miss the regulatory and airspace items that are graded in black and white.

Use official documents as the source of truth. Use practice questions to find holes. Do not skip the testing supplement.

### Cheap paid options if you want more structure

Only buy these if free materials are not getting you to a consistent **85–90%** on mixed timed tests.

- ASA *Remote Pilot Test Prep* + ACS reprint — usually the closest commercial match to FAA style
- A used current-year ASA book or app often costs less than one failed $175 retake
- Pilot Institute / Drone Launch Academy / similar video courses: convenient, not required
- Printed FAA-CT-8080-2H testing supplement (color) if you do not want to study figures on a screen

A failed attempt costs another exam fee and 14 days. A $20–$40 book is cheap insurance; a $300 video course is optional.

### A first-try study plan using only cheap/free tools

**Week 1 — rules and airspace**
- Read Part 107 end to end once, slowly.
- Read ACS Area I and II.
- Take ALC-451.
- Drill airspace class identification on 10 sectional excerpts a day from the testing supplement.

**Week 2 — weather, loading, charts**
- PHAK weather and airport chapters + METAR/TAF practice.
- Density altitude and CG until you can explain them out loud.
- Chart questions daily until you stop missing floor/ceiling and airport-symbol items.

**Week 3 — operations and mixed exams**
- ADM, CRM, night physiology, emergencies, radio, airport markings.
- Two timed 60-question exams.
- Build an error log by ACS code. Restudy only the missed codes.

**Ready-to-schedule standard**
- 85%+ on at least two timed full tests
- Zero panic on sectional figures
- You can recite the numeric limits and reporting thresholds without looking
- You can explain Category 1–4 and night lighting in one minute each

### Exam-day cheap kit
- Government ID
- FTN
- Sleep and food (IMSAFE applies to test day too)
- No brain dumps. They are often wrong and outdated, especially on night ops, over-people categories, and Remote ID.
